%% ABSTRACT
%% ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
\begin{abstract}
Automated bug assignment aims to reduce triage time in software maintenance
by recommending the most suitable developers to fix newly reported bugs.
Traditional approaches rely on static keyword-based heuristics or simple
similarity metrics that fail to capture the nuanced semantic intent of a bug
report.
In this paper we propose a novel pipeline consisting of
\emph{Hybrid Semantic Prototype Weighting} paired with a
\emph{Learning-to-Rank} (LTR) model.
A Weighted Sum Model (WSM) first computes candidate scores using dynamic
weights derived from cosine similarities between the bug embedding and six
curated ``prototype sentences'' that represent ideal developer attributes.
An XGBoost Ranker then refines the candidate order using a 19-dimensional
feature vector --- spanning developer attributes, bug-developer interaction
features, severity flags, and semantic prototype scores --- together with
historical resolution data, and incorporating a confidence-based fallback
mechanism.
Evaluated on four open-source Bugzilla repositories (Eclipse IDE, Mozilla
Firefox, Mozilla Core, and Thunderbird), our LTR approach consistently
outperforms the semantic WSM baseline, achieving Top-1 accuracies ranging from \textbf{23.00\%} (Eclipse IDE) to \textbf{49.00\%} (Mozilla Core),
with up to a \textbf{+13.00pp} improvement over the baseline.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{Bug Assignment, Machine Learning, Natural Language Processing, Learning to Rank, Semantic Embeddings, XGBoost}

%% I. INTRODUCTION

\section{Introduction}

As software systems grow in complexity, bug-tracking repositories (e.g.,
Bugzilla, Jira) receive hundreds of bug reports daily.
Manual triage is a time-consuming and error-prone process.
The \emph{bug assignment problem} involves recommending a ranked list of
candidate developers who possess the right expertise, availability, and
historical context to resolve a given bug efficiently.

While existing approaches often use Term Frequency--Inverse Document
Frequency (TF-IDF)~\cite{salton1988tfidf} or topic modelling combined with classification
algorithms (such as SVM or Na\"{i}ve Bayes)~\cite{cubranic2004hipikat, anvik2006triage}, they commonly struggle with
vocabulary mismatch and synonyms.
Recent work has explored transformer-based embeddings to address these
limitations~\cite{zaidi2022transformers}, as well as ensemble learning
methods for rapid triage in IoT contexts~\cite{yin2018elm}.
Furthermore, traditional heuristics rely on hardcoded keyword lists to
assess aspects like ``urgency'' or ``experience required.''

In this work we introduce a hybrid framework that bridges modern semantic
Natural Language Processing (NLP) with Learning-to-Rank algorithms.
We replace brittle keyword lists with \emph{Semantic Prototype Weighting},
dynamically scoring bug reports against ``ideal'' natural language sentences
using dense transformer embeddings~\cite{reimers2019sentencetransformers}.
Finally, we train an XGBRanker~\cite{chen2016xgboost} to optimise the final
developer rankings using a pairwise learning-to-rank
objective~\cite{liu2009ltr}, ensuring high precision while maintaining
robust fallback behaviour.

\section{Related Work}

Early automated bug triage systems formulated the developer recommendation problem as text categorization. \'{C}ubrani\'{c} and Murphy~\cite{cubranic2004hipikat} and Anvik et al.~\cite{anvik2006triage} applied traditional machine learning classifiers, such as Na\"{i}ve Bayes and Support Vector Machines, to bug report text. Subsequent research recognized that simple lexical matching struggled with vocabulary mismatch. To address this, Xia et al. proposed DevRec~\cite{xia2013devsim}, a composite approach blending text-based similarity and developer activity profiling. This was later extended with TopicMiner-MTM~\cite{xia2017topicminer}, a specialized topic model that explicitly incorporated multi-feature attributes like software components into Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA). While topic models improved context awareness over TF-IDF, they remain computationally expensive to update and struggle to capture deep syntactic semantics compared to modern dense embeddings. Other historical approaches also highlighted the importance of non-textual attributes; for instance, Weiss et al.~\cite{weiss2007msr} demonstrated that metadata like severity strongly predicts bug fix time, motivating our inclusion of fix time as a core developer attribute.

With the advent of deep learning, researchers began utilizing word embeddings and neural networks to extract richer semantic representations. Following the introduction of BERT~\cite{devlin2019bert}, transformer-based architectures became the state-of-the-art for bug triage. Zaidi et al.~\cite{zaidi2022transformers} demonstrated the effectiveness of transformers for developer assignment, particularly in handling newly joined developers. More recently, Dipongkor and Moran~\cite{dipongkor2023transformer} conducted a comprehensive empirical comparison of transformer architectures (BERT, RoBERTa, DeBERTa), confirming that fine-tuned transformer networks achieve superior classification performance on large-scale bug repositories. However, fine-tuning and running inference on large cross-encoders for thousands of historical bugs is often computationally prohibitive in production. To balance semantic richness with retrieval efficiency, our work utilizes Sentence-BERT (SBERT)~\cite{reimers2019sentencetransformers} to generate static dense embeddings, achieving high-fidelity semantic matching without the overhead of end-to-end model fine-tuning.

Rather than treating bug assignment as a flat multi-class classification problem over hundreds of developers, ranking models formulate the task as predicting an ordered list of candidates. The theoretical foundation of Learning-to-Rank (LTR), particularly pairwise ranking, focuses on optimizing the relative order of items~\cite{liu2009ltr}. Burges et al. introduced RankNet~\cite{burges2005lambdarank} and subsequently LambdaMART~\cite{burges2010lambdamart}, which integrates Information Retrieval (IR) metrics directly into gradient boosted decision trees. In the software engineering domain, Mkaouer et al.~\cite{mkaouer2020ltrdevs} successfully applied LTR methodologies specifically to rank developers for bug assignment, demonstrating significant improvements over pointwise classification. Furthermore, industrial-scale evaluations by Jonsson et al.~\cite{jonsson2016deeplearning} and fast triage architectures by Yin et al.~\cite{yin2018elm} have shown that ensemble methods and decision trees are highly robust for large, noisy bug repositories. Our approach builds upon these findings by employing an XGBRanker~\cite{chen2016xgboost} equipped with LambdaMART. We distinguish our framework from prior LTR bug assignment systems by dynamically injecting semantic prototype weights---computed at inference time---directly into the ranker's feature space, avoiding rigid keyword heuristics.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:pipeline} presents the complete end-to-end pipeline.
It consists of eight sequential steps that progressively narrow an open
developer pool to a confident recommendation, combining semantic
retrieval, multi-criteria decision-making, and learning-to-rank.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Automated Bug Assignment Pipeline}
\label{alg:pipeline}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require New bug report $B_{\text{new}}$, historical bugs $\mathcal{H}$,
         semantic prototypes $\mathcal{P}$, pre-trained XGBRanker $\mathcal{M}$
\Ensure  Ranked list of developers $C_{\text{ranked}}$

\LineComment{Step 1: Hybrid Feature Extraction}
\State $E^{\text{new}}_{\text{dense}}  \leftarrow \textsc{SentenceTransformer}(B_{\text{new}})$
\State $E^{\text{new}}_{\text{sparse}} \leftarrow \textsc{TF-IDF}(B_{\text{new}})$

\LineComment{Step 2: Hybrid Similarity Search}
\For{$H_i \in \mathcal{H}$}
  \State $S(B_{\text{new}}, H_i) \leftarrow
    0.5\cdot\cos\!\left(E^{\text{new}}_{\text{dense}},\, E^{H_i}_{\text{dense}}\right)
    + 0.5\cdot\cos\!\left(E^{\text{new}}_{\text{sparse}},\, E^{H_i}_{\text{sparse}}\right)$
\EndFor

\LineComment{Steps 3 \& 4: Candidate Extraction and Hard Filtering}
\State $\mathcal{H}_{\text{top}} \leftarrow \textsc{Top-}K\!\left(S(B_{\text{new}}, \mathcal{H}),\; 20\right)$
\State $C \leftarrow \{\text{developers who successfully resolved } h \in \mathcal{H}_{\text{top}}\}$
\For{$d \in C$}
  \If{$d$ is on leave \textbf{or} $d.\textit{Workload} \geq 0.95$}
    \State $C \leftarrow C \setminus \{d\}$
  \EndIf
\EndFor

\LineComment{Steps 5 \& 6: KNN Affinity and Attribute Matrix}
\For{$d \in C$}
  \State $\mathcal{H}_d \leftarrow \{h \in \mathcal{H} \mid h \text{ was fixed by } d\}$
  \State $A_d \leftarrow \dfrac{1}{|\mathcal{H}_d|}\displaystyle\sum_{h \in \mathcal{H}_d} S(B_{\text{new}}, h)$
  \State $X_d \leftarrow [\mathit{Exp}_d,\; FT_d,\; SR_d,\; WL_d,\; DS_d,\; A_d]$
\EndFor

\LineComment{Step 7: Semantic Prototype Weighting}
\For{$j = 1$ \textbf{to} $6$}
  \State $R_j \leftarrow \dfrac{\cos\!\left(E^{\text{new}}_{\text{dense}},\; E^{P_j}_{\text{dense}}\right) + 1.0}{2.0}$
  \State $W_j \leftarrow \dfrac{\left(R_j \cdot \mathcal{M}^{(j)}_{\text{sev}}\right) + 0.05}
                               {\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{6}\left[\left(R_k \cdot \mathcal{M}^{(k)}_{\text{sev}}\right) + 0.05\right]}$
\EndFor

\LineComment{Step 8: Learning-to-Rank (LTR)}
\For{$d \in C$}
  \State $\textit{Score}_{\text{WSM}}(d) \leftarrow \textsc{MinMax}(X_d)\cdot W \times \textit{ExactMatchMult.}$
  \State $F_d \leftarrow \textsc{Extract19DimFeatures}(X_d, B_{\text{new}}, W)$
  \State $\textit{Score}_{\text{LTR}}(d) \leftarrow \mathcal{M}.\textsc{Predict}(F_d)$
\EndFor
\State $C_{\text{ranked}} \leftarrow \textsc{SortDesc}\;C\;\text{by}\;\textit{Score}_{\text{LTR}}$

\LineComment{Safety Guardrail (Confidence Fallback)}
\State $\Delta \leftarrow \textit{Score}_{\text{LTR}}(C_{\text{ranked}}[1]) - \textit{Score}_{\text{LTR}}(C_{\text{ranked}}[2])$
\If{$\Delta < 0.1$}
  \State $C_{\text{ranked}} \leftarrow \textsc{SortDesc}\;C\;\text{by}\;\textit{Score}_{\text{WSM}}$
\EndIf
\State \Return $C_{\text{ranked}}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}



Our methodology operates through a progressive pipeline that narrows down an open pool of developers to a confidently ranked list. The process begins with hybrid feature extraction, where each incoming bug report is converted into two complementary representations. First, we generate a dense semantic embedding ($E_{\text{dense}}$) using a pre-trained SentenceTransformer model, which captures the underlying meaning and context of the bug description in a 384-dimensional vector. Second, we create a sparse lexical embedding ($E_{\text{sparse}}$) using a TF-IDF vectorizer to capture exact keyword matches. These representations are then used in a hybrid similarity search against historical bugs. We compute a balanced similarity score by equally weighting the cosine similarities of both the dense and sparse embeddings:

\begin{equation}
S(B_{\text{new}}, H_i) = 0.5\cdot\cos\!\left(E^{\text{new}}_{\text{dense}},\,E^{H_i}_{\text{dense}}\right) + 0.5\cdot\cos\!\left(E^{\text{new}}_{\text{sparse}},\,E^{H_i}_{\text{sparse}}\right)
\label{eq:hybrid_sim}
\end{equation}

After calculating these similarities, we extract the top 20 most similar historical bugs and compile a preliminary candidate pool of developers who successfully resolved them. To ensure practicality, we apply a hard filter to remove any developers who are currently on leave or have a workload exceeding a maximum threshold of 95\%. For each remaining candidate, we calculate a topical expertise score, called KNN Affinity ($A_d$), which is the average hybrid similarity between the new bug and the subset of historical bugs ($\mathcal{H}_d$) previously fixed by that specific developer:

\begin{equation}
A_d = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{H}_d|}\sum_{h \in \mathcal{H}_d} S(B_{\text{new}}, h)
\label{eq:knn_affinity}
\end{equation}

We then construct a six-dimensional attribute matrix for each candidate. This matrix includes the developer's total historical fixes (Experience, $\mathit{Exp}_d$), average resolution time in days (Fix Time, $FT_d$), ratio of successful fixes (Success Rate, $SR_d$), current number of open bug assignments (Workload, $WL_d$), expertise in the bug's specific module (Domain Skill, $DS_d$), and their KNN Affinity ($A_d$).

To determine the relative importance of these six attributes dynamically, we use Semantic Prototype Weighting. We define six natural language ``prototype sentences'' ($P_j$), each describing the ideal bug scenario for one specific attribute (detailed in Table~\ref{tab:protos}). We calculate a raw prototype score ($R_j$) by finding the normalized cosine similarity between the dense embedding of the new bug (with its severity label appended) and each prototype sentence:

\begin{equation}
R_j = \frac{\cos\!\left(E^{\text{new}}_{\text{dense}},\;E^{P_j}_{\text{dense}}\right) + 1.0}{2.0}
\label{eq:proto_score}
\end{equation}

These scores are adjusted using a multiplicative severity modifier ($M_{\text{sev}}$) that amplifies relevant prototypes based on the bug's severity level. The final normalized weight vector ($W$) is computed as follows, adding a constant $0.05$ to ensure no attribute weight drops entirely to zero:

\begin{equation}
W_j = \frac{\left(R_j \cdot M^{(j)}_{\text{sev}}\right) + 0.05}{\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{6}\left[\left(R_k \cdot M^{(k)}_{\text{sev}}\right) + 0.05\right]}
\label{eq:weights}
\end{equation}

In the final ranking phase, we employ a Learning-to-Rank (LTR) approach using an XGBRanker model trained with a pairwise objective function. First, a Weighted Sum Model (WSM) baseline computes initial developer scores by taking the dot product of the Min-Max normalized attribute matrix and the dynamic weight vector, applying a $1.1\times$ multiplier for exact component matches:

\begin{equation}
\textit{Score}_{\text{WSM}}(d) = \textsc{MinMax}(X_d) \cdot W \;\times\; \textit{ExactMatchMultiplier}
\end{equation}

Next, we extract a comprehensive 19-dimensional feature vector ($F_d$) for each candidate, encompassing static attributes, bug-developer interaction signals, severity context, and semantic prototype similarities (detailed in Table~\ref{tab:ltrfeats}). The XGBRanker uses these features to generate the primary ranking score ($\textit{Score}_{\text{LTR}}(d)$). To ensure reliability, we include a confidence fallback mechanism. If the LTR model's confidence gap ($\Delta$)—the difference in predicted scores between the top two candidates—is less than 0.1, the system safely reverts to the WSM baseline scores to determine the final assignment:

\begin{equation}
\Delta = \textit{Score}_{\text{LTR}}(C_{\text{ranked}}[1]) - \textit{Score}_{\text{LTR}}(C_{\text{ranked}}[2])
\end{equation}

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

We report \textbf{Top-$N$ Accuracy} — the percentage of test bugs for which
the actual resolver appeared in the Top~$N$ recommendations — for
$N \in \{1, 3, 5\}$.
We also report the \textbf{Mean Reciprocal Rank} (MRR):

\begin{equation}
\text{MRR} = \frac{1}{|Q|}\sum_{i=1}^{|Q|}\frac{1}{\text{rank}_i}
\label{eq:mrr}
\end{equation}

Three structural factors explain the performance gap:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Candidate pool size.} Eclipse contains only 209 developers,
        making the classification problem substantially easier than Mozilla
        Core (2,033 developers) or Firefox (854). A smaller candidate pool
        reduces the probability of confusing similar developers.

  \item \textbf{Training signal per developer.} Eclipse averages 47.8 bugs
        per developer, providing the model with rich historical context to
        learn each developer's expertise profile. In contrast, Thunderbird
        averages only 15.3 bugs per developer, and nearly half (47.5\%) of
        its developers fixed exactly one bug---leaving the model with no
        historical signal to predict these \emph{singleton} developers.
        Figure~\ref{fig:long_tail} visualises this long-tail distribution
        across all four repositories.

  \item \textbf{Component granularity.} Eclipse's 23 well-defined components
        provide a strong routing signal for component-based matching.
        Mozilla Core's 179 components fragment the signal, reducing the
        discriminative power of the \texttt{component\_match} feature.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Concentration Effects}
An additional observation is the extreme developer concentration in the
Mozilla projects. In Mozilla Core, a single developer resolved 40.1\% of all
bugs (34,347 out of 85,552), and in Thunderbird the top developer fixed
44.3\%. This skews the training distribution---the model learns to favour the
dominant developer, but every assignment to a less prolific developer becomes
a ``surprise'' that reduces measured accuracy.

Despite these structural challenges, the LTR model's \emph{relative
improvement} over the WSM baseline remains consistent across all four
projects (ranging from +15\% to +31\% relative), demonstrating that the
learned feature interactions generalise across different project structures
and developer ecosystems.

Recent empirical studies, such as the comprehensive benchmarking of transformer architectures by Dipongkor and Moran~\cite{dipongkor2023transformer}, demonstrate that fine-tuned models like DeBERTa can achieve Top-1 accuracies between 35\% and 45\% in bug triaging. However, these standard approaches formulate the task as a flat multi-class classification problem. This approach suffers from two critical deployment flaws: (1) it cannot gracefully handle the dynamic nature of open-source developer pools---if a developer joins or leaves, the multi-class output layer must be retrained; and (2) it requires immense computational overhead to process the full text through a deep transformer during inference, making it unsuitable for rapid triage SLAs.

Furthermore, standard multi-class formulations require heavily filtering the dataset to remove ``long-tail'' developers (those who only fix a few bugs), as neural networks struggle to learn classes with minimal examples. 

Our Hybrid Semantic Prototype framework overcomes these limitations. By adopting a pairwise Learning-to-Rank (LTR) formulation, our model learns the relational mapping between bugs and developers, making it natively robust to shifting candidate pools and capable of predicting long-tail developers. Furthermore, by decoupling the textual semantics (via lightweight static SBERT embeddings) from the classification logic (via XGBRanker), we achieve a highly competitive Top-3 accuracy of 55.00\% on the challenging, unfiltered Eclipse IDE dataset with a CPU inference latency of just $\sim$150\,ms per bug. This demonstrates that hybrid LTR models offer a vastly more deployable and scalable alternative to massive end-to-end transformers for industrial bug triage.

\section{Conclusion}

This paper presented a modern architecture for automated bug assignment,
replacing rigid keyword heuristics with dense semantic prototypes and
pairwise learning-to-rank.
By combining SentenceTransformers with an XGBoost Ranker, we achieved a
highly robust triage pipeline that generalises across multiple open-source
repositories.

Experimental evaluation across four major projects---Eclipse IDE, Mozilla
Firefox, Mozilla Core, and Thunderbird---demonstrated consistent improvements,
with Top-1 accuracy gains of up to \textbf{+4.5 percentage points} and MRR
improvements of up to \textbf{+0.043} over the semantic WSM baseline.
Our analysis further revealed that developer pool size, historical training
density, and component granularity are critical factors governing assignment
difficulty, providing actionable guidance for practitioners deploying
automated triage in their organisations.

\section{Future Work}

Future work will explore continuous online learning to update the XGBRanker
in real time as developers join or leave the organisation, as well as
extending the prototype vocabulary to cover additional bug taxonomies and
severity gradations. We also plan to investigate developer filtering
strategies (e.g., removing singleton developers from the candidate pool)
to improve prediction accuracy in repositories with highly sparse
contributor distributions.
